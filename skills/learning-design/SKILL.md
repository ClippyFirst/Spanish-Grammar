# Reference Explanation and Optional Pathway Design

## Goal

Help readers understand a grammatical point and discover useful related topics while preserving direct lookup. A curated path is optional navigation, not a course requirement.

## Procedure

1. State the reader's likely question in practical terms: identify a form, distinguish two constructions, interpret a sentence, or understand a caveat.
2. Check whether the explanation answers that question directly before introducing terminology or background.
3. Check prerequisites and links to canonical prerequisite topics; do not require a fixed sequence to access an article.
4. Assess whether examples isolate the target distinction and whether a minimal pair, counterexample, table or short diagnostic cue would help.
5. Identify the smallest useful set of related topics for an optional pathway (for example, prerequisite → central distinction → common edge case → regional variation).
6. Keep the path short, explain why each transition is useful, and allow readers to enter or leave at any point.
7. Check that terminology and examples do not assume unexplained knowledge that is not linked or briefly explained.
8. Recommend a visualization only when a specific relationship is genuinely clearer visually; route that decision through `grammar-visualization-auditor`.
9. Define a testable editorial acceptance criterion, such as whether the page answers the target question, exposes its scope and links to the right canonical explanation.

## Output

Reader question; direct answer quality; prerequisite gaps; useful contrasts/examples; optional topic pathway with rationale; unnecessary detours; acceptance criteria; unresolved evidence questions.

## Guardrails

- Do not impose a mandatory learning route, course levels, tests, scoring, progress tracking, retrieval practice or spaced review.
- Do not invent empirical learning gains or call a page CEFR-aligned without explicit evidence.
- Do not force a visualization, exercise or identical article template onto every topic.
- Distinguish a helpful editorial sequence from a required sequence for readers.
- Use existing `prerequisites`, `contrasts`, `extensions` and `exceptions` fields where appropriate.
- The reader must always be able to use the site as a reference and arrive directly at a specific answer.
