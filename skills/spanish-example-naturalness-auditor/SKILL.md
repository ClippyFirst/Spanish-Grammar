# Spanish Example Naturalness Auditor

## Core distinction

Assess these independently: (1) grammatical well-formedness, (2) plausible interpretation, (3) idiomaticity in context, (4) frequency/distribution, (5) suitability for the learner's level and target rule. One verdict must not stand in for all five.

## Workflow

1. Record the target rule, intended meaning, audience, target variety, register and learning objective.
2. Read each example with enough context to interpret it. Flag context-free sentences whose acceptability depends on an unstated discourse situation.
3. Check morphology, syntax, orthography, collocations, semantic selection and pragmatic assumptions.
4. Check whether the example genuinely isolates the target construction or accidentally tests several new concepts at once.
5. Compare minimal pairs and near-minimal contrasts; explain which meaning or context changes.
6. When naturalness, frequency or regional distribution is material, inspect trustworthy corpus concordances and record corpus, query, filters, date and limitations. Do not fabricate searches or counts.
7. Consider Spanish variation: label a form only as regional, colloquial, formal, dated or uncommon when evidence supports that label.
8. Recommend replacement examples that preserve the teaching objective while improving clarity and authenticity.
9. Run the replacement through orthography, morphology and citation checks where relevant.

## Output

Example and location; separate verdicts for grammaticality/naturalness/context/pedagogical fit; source or corpus evidence; regional/register scope; replacement; confidence.

## Guardrails

- “Sounds odd to me” is a lead for investigation, not sufficient evidence for a categorical claim.
- Corpus attestation does not prove universal naturalness; absence from a search does not prove ungrammaticality.
- Do not invent real-life contexts or corpus provenance.
- Prefer short, authentic examples with enough context to remove accidental ambiguity.
